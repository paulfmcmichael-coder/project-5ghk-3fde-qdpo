John James Bergin

This is Jack's Great War story, gleaned and reconstructed from surviving records and the snippets of memory handed down through his children and grandchildren.

01 — Enlistment

Before Enlistment

Sergeant J.J. Bergin served in the Royal Irish Constabulary in Buncrana before the war, and then in 1916 enlisted in the Royal Inniskilling Fusiliers, Reg. No. 29328. This was only weeks after the Easter Rising though he must have been contemplating enlistment for some time before that.

03 — Before Arras

Before Arras

The map below is an overview of the front lines, showing major changes in the Western Front during the war. Amiens was briefly captured by the Germans in 1914 but quickly retaken. Note the big change in the front lines around the Somme in 1916. The front line in early 1917 ran just east of Arras, but crucially, Monchy-le-Preux was in German hands.

The second map is a Google map view showing areas where the 1st Battalion were billeted or fought, using details from 1st Bn. regimental war diary. In January, the regiment was generally around Amiens. In February and March, it was at the Somme battlefields. In April, it moved to the Arras sector in preparation for the attack on the German Hindenburg line, which ran north to south just to the east of Arras but just west of Monchy-le-Preux.

More than three million men fought in the battle, of whom more than one million were either wounded or killed, making it one of the deadliest battles in human history. Source: Wikipedia, Battle of the Somme

04 — Arras

Arras

Aerial fly-over of Arras — click play to view full screen
Main square in Arras Apr-May 1917 — a town in ruins
Arras had been captured briefly by the Germans in 1914. The town was largely in ruins. Tunnels were dug by the Newfoundlanders and in these tunnels the 1st Battalion sheltered before being posted to the front line near Monchy-le-Preux.
Philip Gibbs writes a vivid description of the Arras tunnels: "Wafts of stale air laden with a sickly stench came out of the vaults. Faint whiffs of poison-gas filtered through the soil above and made men vomit." Source: Philip Gibbs, quoted in Francis Ledwidge: A Life Of The Poet by Alice Curtayne
War Diary of Royal Inniskilling Fusiliers 1st Battalion, May 1917
The war diary of the 1st Bn. is a key document, containing a huge amount of information about what was happening on the ground. It is of course meant to be fairly banal but compared to the horrific reality of trench warfare, it feels almost callous. Only officers were named. Everyone else, including sergeants, were simply noted as O.R., other ranks.
Battle Order no. 14, 06 May — march to Duisans (near Arras)
This is a starting point for the action in which Jack fought and was captured. Bn. Order No. 14 commands the battalion to march on May 13th to Duisans near Arras. Another Order No. 16 commands the regiment onwards to the front lines on the evening of 14/15 May (see below).

05 — Monchy-le-Preux

Monchy-le-Preux

Monchy-le-Preux, 2 April 1917 — a scene of devastation
There wasn't much left of Monchy-le-Preux after Commonwealth forces had captured the village on 11 April. The Germans tried to retake it a few days later, on 14 April but were thrown back.

The front lines were re-established just to the east of Monchy.
The Newfoundlanders regiment fought in three directions against a German force some three-times their number until most Newfoundlanders were killed or wounded. There is a memorial to their bravery in Monchy-le-Preux. Source: https://www.unb.ca/initiatives/vimyridge/newfoundland.html retrieved 8 August 2026
A dressing station near Monchy-le-Preux, 24 April 1917
Monchy-le-Preux, 30 May 1917 — in ruins, dead horses in the foreground
The next map below shows the advances made by the British against the Germans from Arras out to the east to Monchy-le-Preux. Action was bogged down close to Monchy along the Hindenburg Line, a line of fortifications that the Germans retreated to to make their front line more easily defensible.

The photograph above was taken in August 1918 but is a good illustration of the destruction of Monchy-le-Preux. Roads have been cleared. In April 1917, it would have been largely rubble.

Here is Battalion Order No. 16, Jack's last written order passed down.
Battle Order #16 — 14 May 1917
Jack served in Company B as his POW record shows. Along with company A, they were to relieve the Gordons Fusiliers on the night of the 14th May, taking a place on Shrapnel Trench running north to south, between Grape and Canister. Shrapnel was just behind a forward line which in turn was only around 150m from the heavily defended, Hook Trench.

Another part of the regiment (C and D company and part of B), went to an area just north, near a wood called Twin Copse. However, Hook Trench was the main part of the German lines that lay in front of Infantry Hill and the Hindenburg line and since this is where night patrols to reconnoitre Hook Trench were needed, then I think Canister Trench is a far more likely location for Jack's part of Company B.
Jack's Company B moved up to occupy a position near Hook Trench. Summary

06 — Reconnoitre and Capture

Action on 15-18 May 1917

RIF War Diary for 14–19 May 1917

Diary wording for 14-19 May 1917: we can imagine a cool, damp evening. There was a waning moon on these dates.

March from ARRAS to front line + take over from 1st Gordons. Vide Bn. orders No. 16 & dispositions attached. Officers' patrols go out nightly.

Enemy holds HOOK TRENCH. Snipers active & intermittent shelling. 4 killed 8 wounded, 1 missing.

We can imagine Sgt Bergin receiving orders to take part in a night patrol on 18 May, maybe with orders to reach Hook Trench, maybe to capture a German soldier. Knowing that night patrols were being fired on on previous nights, and that others had been lost for the nights before, we can merely try to imagine what Jack thought and felt at these orders.

Records from Commonwealth War Graves Commission show that four men of the 1st Bn. died between 15 and 18 May 1917, and since their bodies were never found, they are memorialised on the Arras Memorial. The eight wounded during this time period, obviously made it back to the trenches at least. That leaves one missing. Jack himself noted his own capture on 19 May. He is the 'missing' man.

Attack [..on 19 May from 19:00..] carried out in accordance with attached B.O. 17. The attack fails owing to severe casualties from M.G. fire. Entire party reach their objective but are outnumbered. Lieut Aitcheson effects a lodgement in HOOK TRENCH with about 20 men but nothing more is heard of them.

Casualties: Capt. Osman, 2/Lt McGrane, Lt Hepworth killed. 2/Lt Somers died of wounds. Lt Glancy & 2/Lt Hornage W. & missing. Lieut Hall missing. Lt Aitcheson W. & missing. 2/Lt Graham wounded. O.R. 192 K, W. & M.

Aerial photo overlaid on Google map showing the effect of artillery
This composite image shows the ground east of Monchy-le-Preux, churned and turned by artillery into a cratered, blasted landscape. Canister Trench is visible roughly in the middle of the image. Hook Trench is marked on the right. A newspaper item, published some weeks later, reported Jack's capture and noted that he lay in a trench (Hook Trench, or perhaps a small forward lookout or machine gun branch trench running off at right angles) for twelve hours before he had attempted an escape.

A note on interpretationIt was here, sometime in the morning of the 19th May, that his movements were noticed. He was taken prisoner. He would have known that a major British artillery barrage was due that day so he had to do something. Many had died in the days before and many more would die that night. Jack, playing dead in a trench during those desperate twelve hours, was not one of them.

Body Density Map of the trenches
Hundreds of men were lost in the failed attack of the 19th May on Hook Trench. The map above probably underestimates the number of dead by a wide margin. Mass graves, isolated unmarked remains found later during concentration work, and men whose graves were destroyed or lost to further shelling could all be undercounted. The battle for Hook Trench and the German defences beyond that was a bloodbath.
Jack Bergin's own handwriting on page 176 of a book on the poet Francis Ledwidge.
This page from Maureen Bergin's copy of the Francis Ledwidge biography describes the tunnels in Arras, the cellars of Monchy-le-Preux and the rivers of blood shed for little advance. A scribbled line was added by Jack himself where the text refers to an area east of Monchy-le-Preux. In his own handwriting, a note in the bottom margin tells of his own capture on 19th May.
Source: Francis Ledwidge: A Life Of The Poet, p. 176, by Alice Curtayne
Commonwealth War Graves Commission (CWGC) records show that four 1st Battalion men died during the nightly patrols 15-18 May 1917. One was Private James Gibson from Belfast. They were never found and so they are memorialised on the main British memorial in Arras. James Gallagher and John McCullough are listed as dying on 18 April.

A note on interpretationI'm sure that Jack knew these men. It would be plausible and maybe likely, to say that the two others were out on the same patrol as Jack. Since their bodies were not found, they must have died closer to German lines than British ones. This, together with the fact that Jack lay in a German trench for twelve hours before attempting to escape, paints a slightly different picture: the three of them made it to Hook Trench, and it was not snipers but direct fire that killed the two other men. In the confusion and the darkness, Jack was able to 'play dead' while the bodies of his two comrades lay nearby.

For the period 15–18 May, four men died, matching the diary entry
James Mullen listed as wounded, died in a military hospital on 18 June 1917
James Mullen died on 18 June 1917. He was from Ballina and it seems that he had joined up on the same day as Jack and Hugh Mullan at the same enlistment office. He was wounded sometime in 1917 after arriving in France, but it's not too hard to believe that he fought with the 1st Battalion alongside Jack and Hugh and his wounds occurred during these days of action around Monchy.

After Dülmen, Jack was moved at one point to a camp near the town of Soltau, Lower Saxony. Nearest city, Hannover. It was one of the largest WW1 camps and housed mainly other-ranks Russian and Eastern European POWs.

A note on interpretationI found no records indicating whether Jack lived solely in the main camp or was in effect a farm-labourer outside the camp. A photograph from this period shows Jack in a uniform so living in the camp seems more likely than not.

Jack Bergin and Jerry Mahony in Soltau Camp, 1918
Jerry Mahony's ICRC arrival card — captured at Ypres, Nov 1917
Jerry Mahony's ICRC record card has a lot of details from which we can confirm his identity. He was born in Sneem, Co. Kerry in 1893. He was captured at Ypres on 10 November 1917 (which was the first day of the Battle of Passchendaele, the 3rd Battle of Ypres). He was transferred to Soltau from Dülmen about a month later, so we might speculate that Jack Bergin spent a month at Dülmen too.

A note on interpretationHis capture was Jerry's good fortune too. 500,000 were killed and wounded during this battle.

1926 Census record — Jerry is a farmer with a family in Kilconnell, Galway
Jerry moved to Galway on his return from war and was a farmer in Kilconnell, Co. Galway. He married Kathleen and had three children Gerard, Stephen and Mary.
Gerard MacMichael recalls a visit with Jack and Maureen Bergin to Co. Down, to a 'friend of Jack's from the war.' His name was Bob McCandless. From National Archives War Medal records, there was a Robert McCandless who was in the Royal Inniskilling Fusilliers. It might well be the same person. 10 — The Long Way Home

The Long Way Home

In May 2027, it will be 110 years since Jack went out on that night patrol. Enemy gunfire left his comrades killed or wounded. There is no doubt he fought back. He lay in or near Hook Trench for twelve hours, hoping for an escape back to his own lines. This saved him from a different fate.

His journey back to Belfast happened soon after the Armistice, but after what he had been through, we can also say that he had been as far away from home as a human can get, far off in that place of almost mindless destruction and violence. He went back to Belfast to his parents' house on Eliza Street (now gone) and picked up the pieces of his old life. He married Kate Maguire and they had five children, Hugh, Ita, Gerard, Maureen and Aidan, and lived in St James Drive along the Falls Road. He worked at the Customs Office until he retired (his RIC and war record were an advantage for a Catholic back then).

Jack and Kate some time before the war. Around 1912 perhaps.
Jack in Customs Officer uniform, Belfast

His five children went on to have twenty grandchildren and they in turn, 53 great-grandchildren, a total of seventy-eight descendants in three generations. A fourth generation is still being added to.

The two years of Jack's war were a tumultuous and dreadful time. He never really spoke about his experiences, a word here and there, a note in the margins of a book. The facts are written down on paper, downloaded at the click of a button, but a clear picture remains elusive. Let these fugitive pieces of the past that we have gathered stand as a memorial to Jack's life, and to Sgt. Bergin's six dark days in May 1917.

A GREAT WAR STORY — J.J. "JACK" BERGIN, SERVICE No. 29328, ROYAL INNISKILLING FUSILIERS.
